Discussion
Our results demonstrate that investing in on-campus safe vegetable access is financially justifiable for garment companies in Vietnam, with FarmStand offering the greatest expected benefit and ConnectFarm a lower-risk entry point. Both interventions improve worker food environments relative to DoNothing — FarmStand generating positive food environment outcomes in 99% of simulation runs and ConnectFarm in 88% — providing a robust justification for action even when full financial returns remain uncertain. The central finding for practice is that the farmer revenue-sharing arrangement is simultaneously the primary driver of FarmStand's financial case and the single variable most worth reducing uncertainty about, and the pilot directly addressed this gap. By integrating food environment assessment, probabilistic decision analysis, and a real-world pilot, this study shows that rigorous decision-support tools can guide inclusive food safety investments even in data-sparse, rapidly urbanising contexts.

Food Environment Constraints on Safe Vegetable Access
To situate our findings within the broader literature, we relate them to the global food environment framework developed by  TURNER ET AL., (2018), which conceptualizes the food environment as the interface between individuals and the wider food system in rapidly changing urban contexts in low- and middle-income countries (LMIC). In this framework, food acquisition is shaped by the interaction of two domains: an external domain, encompassing food availability, prices, vendor and product characteristics, and regulatory conditions; and a personal domain, reflecting consumers’ perceptions of accessibility, affordability, convenience, and desirability. Our findings illustrate the possibility of interventions to influence vegetable consumption in the food environments of LMI urban consumers in Hanoi. Our expert-based food environment mapping highlights how these dimensions are experienced by consumers not in isolation, but through simultaneous trade-offs across multiple food sources. The coexistence of wet markets, grocery stores, supermarkets and mini-marts, street vendors, online platforms, home production, and exchange or gifting reflects the interface between the external domain (accessibility and convenience) and the personal domain (affordability and food safety). Informal outlets such as street vendors and wet markets tend to perform well in terms of affordability and convenience, yet raise concerns regarding food safety, whereas safety-controlled stores and supermarkets are perceived as safer but less accessible due to higher prices, limited spatial reach, or misaligned operating hours.
The findings underscore the persistent and functional role of informal and semi-formal food channels in the vegetable food environment for LMI consumers. These channels persist because they remain closely aligned with everyday needs, particularly spatial accessibility, temporal flexibility, and trust embedded in familiar social relations. In contrast, there are notable mismatches between existing modern retail configurations, which are often designed around higher-income consumption patterns, and the lived realities of LMI urban consumers. Such mismatches include operating hours that do not align with work schedules, physical distance from residential or workplace locations, and price structures that constrain regular purchasing.
The consumer-centered, polycentric structure emerging from our analysis further suggests that vegetable acquisition follows multiple, context-dependent pathways, rather than a linear transition from production to consumption. Different food sources are activated depending on household circumstances, mobility, and tolerance of food safety risks, highlighting the limitations of supermarket-centered modernization strategies when implemented in isolation. The FarmStand and ConnectFarm interventions modelled in this study respond directly to these structural mismatches: both bring safe vegetable access into or near the workplace, within the rhythms of the working day, rather than requiring workers to navigate an already fragmented external food environment. The DoNothing baseline in the model implicitly reflects the existing informal market structure — accessible but food-safety uncertain — against which both interventions compete. Overall, the findings reinforce the importance of context-sensitive approaches to food system transformation that complement, rather than displace, existing informal supply channels.
Decision Analysis and Structured Assessment of Interventions
Decision analysis served in this study not as an optimization tool but as a structured framework for making assumptions, trade-offs, and uncertainties explicit and discussable. The probabilistic model was developed iteratively with stakeholders, converting qualitative judgments about costs, benefits, and risks into a quantitative structure that could support dialogue rather than dictate conclusions. This approach aligns with the use of Monte Carlo decision analysis in agricultural and food system contexts (LUEDELING ET AL., 2021; WHITNEY ET AL., 2018), where identifying which uncertainties matter most — and directing learning efforts there — is often more valuable than generating a precise prediction.
Prior research on the relationship between agricultural cooperatives and private-sector companies has rarely accounted for the structural uncertainty inherent in such arrangements — particularly the variable and conditional nature of farmer revenue sharing and its dependence on trust, contract enforcement, and cooperative capacity. Applying probabilistic decision analysis to this institutional relationship makes these uncertainties visible and tractable. By quantifying the range of possible outcomes and identifying which variables most influence the decision, the model enables more grounded conversations between companies and cooperatives about expectations, risk allocation, and the conditions under which investment is worthwhile.
The five-year NPV results reveal a clear contrast between the two interventions. FarmStand has a median NPV of 81 million VND (90% CI: −256 to +527 million VND; probability positive: 63%) — a meaningful expected return for a company of GARCO 10’s scale, where monthly worker wages average roughly 6 million VND per person. The wide confidence interval reflects genuine uncertainty about whether and at what scale the farmer revenue-sharing arrangement will materialise. ConnectFarm clusters tightly near zero (median: 0.47 million VND; 59% positive), financially neutral but with minimal downside risk. This contrast maps onto a familiar investment trade-off: FarmStand requires supply chain capacity and risk tolerance, while ConnectFarm suits companies seeking a low-cost entry point before deeper engagement.
The PLS sensitivity analysis reveals that the two interventions are driven by structurally different pathways. For FarmStand, the dominant positive drivers are farmer revenue shared with the company and the probability of farmer reputation gain, reflecting that the financial case rests primarily on the revenue-sharing arrangement with the cooperative — not solely on the food environment benefits that motivated the intervention. Staff and operational costs are the main negative drivers, confirming that labour investment is the primary source of financial risk. For ConnectFarm, the dominant positive drivers are the food nutrition base value and the health response to the food environment. This is a structural feature of the model: ConnectFarm generates no farmer revenue share or campus amenity benefit, so its entire return depends on the food environment pathway — improvements in worker nutrition translating to reduced absenteeism and productivity gains. The prominence of organizational and management risk variables as negative drivers underscores that ConnectFarm's food environment benefits depend on service reliability; a poorly organised or intermittent supply connection rapidly erodes the nutrition pathway. These findings strengthen the case for longitudinal data on the relationship between vegetable access, dietary quality, and worker productivity in the Vietnamese factory context.
The EVPI analysis sharpens the interpretation of the PLS findings. For FarmStand, farmer revenue shared with the company has an EVPI of 9.1 million VND — approximately 11% of the median NPV — meaning a decision-maker could rationally invest up to that amount to eliminate uncertainty about this variable before committing. All other FarmStand variables have EVPI below 0.14 million VND, indicating that the revenue-sharing arrangement is the only knowledge gap that materially affects the decision. Critically, the pilot directly addressed this uncertainty: the tripartite agreement signed between GARCO 10, the Bac Hong cooperative, and the research team — and the 25 tons of vegetables subsequently sold — represents precisely the kind of evidence needed to tighten the input range for this variable. The pilot thus functioned as a structured information-gathering exercise targeted at the highest-value uncertainty in the model, consistent with the logic of EVPI-guided adaptive management. For ConnectFarm, all variables have EVPI values near zero, reflecting that the food environment pathway determines the outcome regardless of which specific parameter values are realised. This robustness is not a limitation — it indicates that the ConnectFarm decision does not require additional information investment before implementation.
Iterative Learning Through Modeling and Piloting
The relationship between the model and the pilot in this study was bidirectional. Before implementation, the model served as a boundary object — a structured artefact that made implicit assumptions explicit and created a common reference point for dialogue between the research team, GARCO 10, and the Bac Hong cooperative. Discussions about the model’s cost and benefit structure surfaced expectations that neither party had previously articulated, including anticipated sales volumes, worker uptake rates, and the scope of the company’s operational role. This pre-pilot modelling phase reduced the risk of misaligned expectations and helped both partners calibrate the practical scope of the intervention. After implementation, the pilot generated observations that challenged several model assumptions. Actual sales volumes and per-unit prices allowed input ranges to be updated with empirical values; new structural variables — notably the role of the research facilitator and the cooperative’s learning curve as a direct retailer — were identified as important and incorporated into the revised model. This cycle, in which modelling informs implementation and implementation refines the model, reflects an adaptive management approach appropriate to complex food system interventions where uncertainty is irreducible at the outset.
The theoretical model developed to guide a company's decision on investing in an on-premise direct vegetable sales model has illuminated numerous factors consistent with existing literature. These factors predominantly relate to established consumer behavior theories and the complex interplay within the broader food system. However, through the practical operationalization of the model in this study, an equally critical yet less emphasized success factor emerged: the facilitator. This encompasses the crucial support provided by research units and project partners, which proved pivotal in promoting the operational model. Additionally, the study highlighted the importance of specialized skills acquired by cooperatives as they embraced a new role as retailers directly engaging with customers. This study significantly contributes by clarifying and providing a concrete framework for applying DA in a practical setting. Concurrently, a novel actor, the private sector entity, has been rigorously analyzed within the model, encompassing its comprehensive roles, associated costs, benefits, and inherent risks. This detailed analysis elucidates the critical participatory and supportive role this group plays in fostering and promoting an effective short food supply chain.
Implications, Limitations, and Future Research
The results underscore the need for food safety strategies that are sensitive to everyday food environments and inclusive of the lived routines of LMI workers, rather than relying solely on formal regulatory compliance. Vietnam’s food safety challenges — including documented overuse of agricultural chemicals in vegetable production and the resulting erosion of consumer trust (HOI ET AL., 2016; HA ET AL., 2020) — have persisted despite regulatory attention, in part because they are embedded in economic structures that make safe vegetables financially inaccessible to lower-income consumers. Industrial companies represent an underutilised leverage point: as employers of large numbers of LMI workers, they can facilitate safe vegetable access at scale, align procurement with certified producers, and extend quality-assured supply chains to populations that cannot independently access supermarkets or certified stores. Importantly, the business case for doing so is positive in expectation: the model indicates that FarmStand generates a median NPV of 81 million VND over five years, with 63% of simulations positive, while food environment benefits are positive in 99% of runs regardless of the financial outcome. Even for companies that are risk-averse about the financial uncertainty of FarmStand, the food environment argument alone — improved nutrition, reduced absenteeism, and worker loyalty — constitutes a justifiable investment. The model demonstrates that framing this as a productivity investment, rather than solely a welfare or CSR commitment, substantially strengthens the case for company engagement and aligns company incentives with worker welfare outcomes.

Based on the totality of model results and pilot evidence, we recommend that companies with sufficient supply chain capacity and risk tolerance prioritise the FarmStand model, while those at earlier stages of engagement consider ConnectFarm as a low-cost entry point that can be scaled once foundational cooperative relationships are established. In both cases, securing explicit contractual commitments around farmer revenue sharing and delivery volumes before investment is the single highest-value action a decision-maker can take.
Nevertheless, this study is subject to several limitations. First, data collection was constrained by the sensitivity of financial and operational information, meaning that critical inputs — particularly the base monetary values for each food environment component (accessibility, convenience, safety, and nutrition) — relied on expert elicitation and qualitative judgment rather than observed data, which may limit the precision of financial estimations. Second, the food environment score uses an unweighted additive structure, implying equal and substitutable contributions from each component — a simplification consistent with composite index methodology (TURNER ET AL., 2018; DOWNS ET AL., 2020) but one that may not reflect the relative importance of each dimension for Vietnamese factory workers specifically. Third, the accessibility risk modifier is applied independently to both the food environment score and the worker time-savings pathway, which may modestly overstate the compounding effect of accessibility disruptions on FarmStand NPV. Fourth, the five-year time horizon captures near-term financial flows but may understate the long-term nutritional and health benefits of sustained improved food access — a particularly important caveat for ConnectFarm, whose entire return depends on dietary change sustained over time. Fifth, the analysis focused on two specific partners within a defined urban industrial context; findings may not transfer directly to other factory or city settings without adaptation to local wage structures, supply chain configurations, and food environment conditions. Future research should pursue longitudinal evaluation of food environment and productivity outcomes, test the framework in other urban industrial contexts in Vietnam and beyond, and develop empirical estimates of the health-productivity response coefficient — currently the least well-constrained parameter linking food environment improvements to worker outcomes.
